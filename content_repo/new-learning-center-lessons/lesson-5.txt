[center]Understanding Performance Analysis[/center]


[indent]After starting to practice telepathy, the question becomes, “How am I doing?” This question is answered by looking at your performance history to date and assessing whether it reflects telepathic ability, and whether it is improving, declining, or stable. Perhaps the best way to know whether you are experiencing telepathy is to simply introspect and remember whether the fleeing images that you noticed in your mind’s eye were ‘hits’ or not.[/indent]

[indent]The Performance Reports perform statistical analysis on your responses, which is the scientifically acceptable way of determining whether data reflects the presence of a "statistically significant effect" or not. It does so by computing whether the same data could have been produced simply by pure chance. This approach has much strength, but it also has weaknesses.[/indent]

[center]THE TROUBLE WITH STATISTICS[/center]

[indent]Statistics is based on computing whether an effect that could have happened by random chance has occurred. Let’s say you work with this app and get 8 trials correct in a row and then 1 wrong. If you are doing a task with two choices, for example, "one cone" or "many cones", each choice has a 50% chance of being right or wrong by chance alone. Statistical analysis would determine that your performance of 8 correct in a row and then 1 wrong would have a 1.95 % probability of achieving this result purly from chance guessing. Since this is greater than 1%, if it happened only once, it is often rejected as being convincing proof of a significant effect.[/indent]

[indent]But the reality might be that you experienced true telepathy in the first 8 trials and then became tired or distracted on the ninth trial. Or the person sending to you might have been distracted on the ninth trial. Statistics says that there is not enough evidence that telepathy is real in this case. But the reality may well be that there was plenty of true telepathy in the trials that were answered correctly.[/indent]

[indent]IF YOU CORRECTLY AND REPEATEDLY PERCEIVED EVEN FLEETING VISUAL FEATURES OF WHAT THE SENDER WAS SENDING, YOU KNOW WHAT YOU SAW, and in such a case the statistics do not tell the correct story.[/indent]

How To Read Statistical Significance

[indent]The main number to understand in the concept of “statistical significance” is called the “P value.” For example, a report might say,[/indent]

[indent]Summary: Total trials = 24, Chance score = 12, Your score = 13, Telepathic significance, P = .41941.
The probability that you would get this score by chance alone is 41.94%.[/indent]

[indent]The **smaller** the P value, the more your score would be unlikely to occur by chance alone. But statistical significance is only part of the story. The number of completed trials also matters.[/indent]

[indent]A small block of trials can sometimes produce a mathematically significant result just by luck. For that reason, the right approach is to treat small-sample significance more cautiously than significance based on a larger block of completed trials.[/indent]

[indent]The more completed trials you have, the more stable and trustworthy the result becomes. With very few completed trials, a significant result may be interesting, but it is not yet very persuasive. With a moderate number of completed trials, a significant result becomes more meaningful, but with a large block of completed trials, a strong significant result becomes much more persuasive and deserves serious attention.[/indent]

[indent]The performance analysis asks two main questions:[/indent]

[indent]1. Is the result statistically unlikely to be due to chance?[/indent]

[indent]2. Is the result based on enough completed trials to be taken seriously?[/indent]


How This Analysis Interprets Sample Size Persuasiveness

[indent]A result can be mathematically significant without yet being practically persuasive. As a practical guide, the analysis treats sample size roughly as follows:[/indent]

[indent]Fewer than 20 completed trials: Too little data for a meaningful interpretation.[/indent]

[indent]20 to 49 completed trials: Results may be mathematically interesting, but the sample is still modest.[/indent]

[indent]50 or more completed trials: Results become much more practically persuasive.[/indent]

[indent]A result stronger than P < 0.01 with 50 or more completed trials provides strong evidence that deserves serious attention and replication. And any result stronger than P < 0.001 with 25 or more completed trials also provides pursuasive evidence.[/indent]


Important Caution

[indent]Statistical significance does not prove that telepathy is real. It means only that the observed set of scores would be unlikely under the "null hypothesis" meaning, "under chance alone."[/indent]

[indent]A convincing result should ideally show:[/indent]

[indent]-	a low P value[/indent]

[indent]-	enough completed trials[/indent]

[indent]-	stability over time[/indent]

[indent]-	replication across additional sessions[/indent]

[indent]The discussion above showed how statistics can be used to mathematically rule out effects that can be explained by random chance alone. This does not really prove that effects are “real” or not. It just says whether the data could be explained by random chance – and that’s a pretty strong statement, but as seen in “the problem with statistics,” statistics can also hide real effects.[/indent]

[indent]When what may happen can be explained by, "The subject did well in the first group of trials but then got tired and did not do well in the second group" – the P value may not be significant while the truth may be that a real effect did occur, but not enough 'hits' occurred to satisfy statistical significance.[/indent]

[indent]Remember, if one wants to prove that "white crows exist" it takes only a **single white crow** to prove beyond a doubt that the answer is "yes."[/indent]

[indent]But the most interesting report is the Visualization Report. This report takes the raw data and plots it on a graph which shows the boundaries of chance performance. If you start producing data that rises above the upper boundary of chance performance and stays there, that data indicates that something other than pure chance is occurring. [indent]In the Level 1 practice trials, the odds of getting a response correct by chance alone is 50% (one half). So every time you get a response right, the graph goes up by one half; and every time you get a response wrong, the graph goes down by one half. If, on the average, you do better than chance, this plot will creep upwards.This graph plots "CUMULATIVE DATA."[/indent]

[indent]If this graph goes above the shaded zone, it means that statistically there is less than a 5% chance that the data reflects chance alone. The longer and farther from the edge of the shaded zone that the graph data is plotted, the less and less chance there is that it can be explained by chance alone.[/indent] 

Practice Effect

[indent]To see if, over time, you are getting better with practice, you need to compare the RATE at which the cumulative graph creeps up. To analyze whether you are improving with practice, look at the rate at which the cumulative graph is increasing at some early date, and compare it with the rate at chich it is increasing at a later date. If it is increasing faster on the average later than it was increasing earlier, then yes, performance is improving with practice. Practically speaking, in order to see if there is a practice effect, you need a relatively large number of trials. [/indent]

[indent]Practice effects are analyzed statistically in the "Advanced Detail" report. The best way to see if practice is improving performance is with a "scatter graph". If you get a response correct, plot a +1/2. If you get a response incorrect, plot a -1/2. Then draw a best fit straight line through all the points. If, as time goes on, you start getting more +1/2 scores than when you started, the line will trend upwards, showing a positive effect with practice.[/indent]

Interpretation of the Data

The text provided on these pages summarizes what the data mean. In the 16-trial example discussed here, it says that:

1. The probability that you would get this score by chance alone is 10.51%.

2. Effect size: 0.38 sigma (0.38 standard deviations above chance). This is a strong effect.

Effect size is an estimate of how strong the telepathic effect itself appears to be. This is important because the P value and the number of completed trials do not tell exactly the same story. The P value tells how unlikely a result would be if chance alone were operating. But if more and more trials are added, even a weak effect can eventually produce a very impressive P value. In other words, a large number of trials can make a small effect look statistically dramatic. Effect size helps correct for this by estimating the strength of the underlying effect itself rather than how impressive the statistics look simply because the number of trials is large. Ideally, if the same level of performance continues as more trials are added, the effect size stays about the same even while the P value becomes more and more impressive. In this example, an effect size of 0.38 sigma means that the result is estimated to be 0.38 standard deviations above chance expectation, which is a strong effect. However, because this estimate is based on only 16 trials, it is still not very stable. With small numbers of trials, effect-size estimates can vary considerably, so more data are needed to see whether the effect size truly holds.

3. Interpretation:  Given the performance in these 16 trials, there are still too few completed scored trials for a meaningful overall interpretation.

4. Practice trend: more trials are needed before a meaningful practice trend can be estimated.

5. Projection to significance: If performance continued at approximately this same level, the result would first fall below P < .01 (1%) at about 39 completed scored trials, projected P = .00959.

What this means is that if performance remained at the same level, after 39 trials, the probability that chance would produce the same results is one percent. This is considered a "statistically significant" result by many.

6. Projection to high persuasiveness: if performance continued at approximately this same level, the result would first enter the highly persuasive range at about 68 completed scored trials, projected P = .000993.

This says that if performance continued at the same level for 68 trials, the probability of the data reflecting chance is one in one thousand. If anyone could do this twice in a row, science would have to acknowledge that what is commonly called "telepathy" is real.

7. Conclusion: Too few completed Level 1 trials are available to interpret conclusively.  

These sixteen trials show promise, but are not enough to be statistically convincing. 


To Think About

[indent]You can create custom reports where you can specify the starting and ending trial number for the data to be visualized and analyzed statistically, and give that report a name you specify. But when you single out a particularly good run and turn it into a report, this is considered in statistics to be a “post hoc analysis” ("hoc" is Latin for "this"). Such data reports don’t have full credibility because you could just be picking out a particularly good run of chance correct responses, so the post hoc data could be focusing on random data that just looks significant. However, if there are legitimate reasons for doing excluding some responses, for example, if before a formal study you give subjects 10 practice trials, you have a legitimate reason for excluding those trials – especially if you state beforehand in a filed document that you are going to eliminate the first 10 trials as "practice trials" in the data analysis.[/indent]

[indent]And, in a report you can hover the cursor over any individual point and its SESSION | UTC DATE / TIME | LEVEL | SCORE will be displayed.[/indent]
